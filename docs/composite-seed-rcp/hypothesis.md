## Abstract

This study investigates whether finite aggregates of rhombic dodecahedral (RD) cells can reproduce boundary conditions associated with selected four-dimensional closure geometries through a construction termed Radial Cell Projection (RCP).

The central question is:

> Can RD aggregates reproduce the boundary conditions of 4D closure geometries and thereby enable exact or asymptotically near-perfect closure?

The investigation separates two otherwise conflated notions of closure. **Positional closure** concerns whether the exposed cell data occupy the correct boundary hyperplanes or, in the three-dimensional representation, the corresponding effective seed-face planes. **Orientational closure** concerns whether the microscopic facet normals reproduce the corresponding target mirror or face-normal orientations.

Preliminary computational results for cube- and octahedron-based constructions indicate a striking two-part structure: exposed facet centroids can lie exactly on the intended effective boundary planes at every tested resolution, while the individual RD facet normals retain a resolution-independent angular offset from the corresponding target directions. This suggests that increasing aggregate resolution does not necessarily convert microscopic geometric mismatch into exact orientational agreement.

The resulting distinction is important. Under a coarse composite-seed interpretation, the RD aggregate may reproduce the positional boundary of a target 4D construction exactly. Under a fine mirror-system interpretation, the same aggregate may fail to reproduce the target orientation system even in the limit of increasing resolution.

The present formulation therefore treats the positional result as a computational finding to be formally proved from the lattice-selection rules, treats the angular results as results requiring reproducible vector derivation, and leaves open a separate question: whether the fixed-angle microscopic mirror system generated by the RD construction itself defines a finite reflection group or another non-standard 4D structure.

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Motivation and context

The rhombic dodecahedron is a particularly relevant three-dimensional cell for this investigation because it is the Voronoi cell associated with face-centred-cubic packing and is a space-filling polyhedron. It possesses twelve rhombic faces and fourteen vertices, with a geometry naturally related to the cubic and octahedral direction systems. *(MathWorld)*

The present work asks whether finite aggregates of such cells can encode boundary conditions associated with higher-dimensional closure geometries.

The proposed mechanism is Radial Cell Projection (RCP): a mapping in which geometric information carried by a three-dimensional RD aggregate is interpreted as a lower-dimensional representation of a higher-dimensional seed or boundary system.

The motivation is not merely to approximate a four-dimensional object visually. The stronger question is whether discrete three-dimensional aggregation can reproduce mathematically meaningful boundary conditions of a four-dimensional closure exactly, or whether any apparent closure necessarily contains an irreducible microscopic geometric error.

This distinction becomes particularly important because a macroscopic boundary may be geometrically exact even when its constituent microscopic facets do not individually possess the orientation of the macroscopic boundary.

### 1.2 Core question

The principal research question is:

> Can RD aggregates reproduce the boundary conditions of 4D closure geometries and thereby enable exact or asymptotically near-perfect closure?

The question is deliberately neutral. It does not assume that RD aggregates can reproduce four-dimensional closure.

Three controlled families provide useful test cases:

- cube-based aggregation;
- octahedron-based aggregation;
- tetrahedron-based aggregation.

The cube and octahedron cases are particularly useful because they correspond, within standard four-dimensional regular-polytope theory, to structures involving the 8-cell (tesseract), 24-cell, and related duality structures. The regular 4-polytopes include the 5-cell, 8-cell, 16-cell, 24-cell, 120-cell, and 600-cell, with Coxeter types $A_4$, $B_4$, $F_4$, and $H_4$ occurring among their symmetry groups. *(Wikipedia)*

The precise correspondence between an RD aggregate and any particular 4-polytope, however, is a property of the proposed RCP construction and must therefore be defined explicitly rather than assumed from the dimensional analogy.

### 1.3 Principal preliminary finding

The current computational evidence suggests a separation between two forms of closure.

**Positional closure.** For the cube and octahedron constructions tested so far, exposed facet centroids appear to occupy exact boundary layers corresponding to the selected effective seed geometry.

**Orientational closure.** The microscopic facet normals appear to retain fixed angular offsets relative to the corresponding effective boundary directions.

The reported cube value is

$$\theta_{\text{cube}} = 45^\circ.$$

The reported octahedral value is

$$\theta_{\text{oct}} = \arcsin\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\right) \approx 35.2643897^\circ.$$

These values should be regarded as computational results requiring explicit derivation from the RCP coordinate definitions, rather than as consequences that follow merely from the existence of an RD lattice.

The resulting "two flat lines" pattern is therefore the principal phenomenon to be tested rigorously:

$$\text{positional error} = 0$$

while

$$\text{orientational error} = \text{constant} \neq 0.$$

## 2. Background and Definitions

### 2.1 Rhombic-dodecahedral lattice

The rhombic dodecahedron has twelve congruent rhombic faces and fourteen vertices. One standard coordinate realization has vertices

$$(\pm1, \pm1, \pm1)$$

together with

$$(\pm2, 0, 0), \quad (0, \pm2, 0), \quad (0, 0, \pm2).$$

It is the dual of the cuboctahedron and is a space-filling polyhedron. *(MathWorld)*

The RD lattice used here should be specified explicitly in terms of:

1. cell-centre coordinates;
2. allowed centre-to-centre offsets;
3. facet-centroid coordinates;
4. facet-normal directions;
5. the coordinate normalization adopted by the RCP implementation.

In particular, the commonly occurring $\langle110\rangle$-type directions must be distinguished carefully from the $\langle111\rangle$ directions associated with body diagonals of the cubic coordinate frame. This distinction becomes essential in the tetrahedral experiment.

### 2.2 Radial Cell Projection

For purposes of this study, Radial Cell Projection (RCP) is defined as a mapping from a finite three-dimensional RD aggregate to an effective higher-dimensional seed/boundary representation.

The construction must specify:

$$\mathcal{R}: \mathcal{A}_{\text{RD}} \rightarrow \mathcal{S}_{\text{eff}},$$

where $\mathcal{A}_{\text{RD}}$ is the selected RD aggregate and $\mathcal{S}_{\text{eff}}$ is its effective seed representation.

Two geometrically distinct data sets are retained.

**Effective seed surface.** The first is a coarse representation obtained from the positions of exposed facet centroids.

For a boundary class $B_i$, let

$$C_i = \{c_{i1}, c_{i2}, \dots, c_{in_i}\}$$

be the corresponding exposed centroid set. A best-fit plane is then

$$P_i: \quad n_i \cdot x = d_i.$$

If every $c \in C_i$ satisfies

$$n_i \cdot c = d_i,$$

then the centroid set is exactly coplanar. This is the coarse positional object.

**Micro-facet mirror system.** The second data set retains the individual RD facet normals:

$$N_i = \{\nu_{i1}, \nu_{i2}, \dots, \nu_{in_i}\}.$$

These normals need not coincide with the normal $n_i$ of the effective boundary plane.

This distinction is fundamental:

$$\text{effective boundary normal} \neq \text{individual facet normal}$$

in the general case.

## 3. Target Four-Dimensional Closure Geometries

### 3.1 Standard four-dimensional reference structures

The relevant regular four-dimensional polytopes include:

| 4-polytope | Schläfli symbol | Coxeter type |
|---|---|---|
| 5-cell | $\{3,3,3\}$ | $A_4$ |
| 8-cell / tesseract | $\{4,3,3\}$ | $B_4$ |
| 16-cell | $\{3,3,4\}$ | $B_4$ |
| 24-cell | $\{3,4,3\}$ | $F_4$ |
| 120-cell | $\{5,3,3\}$ | $H_4$ |
| 600-cell | $\{3,3,5\}$ | $H_4$ |

These structures and their dual relationships are standard. *(Wikipedia)*

The important methodological point is that these standard polytopes provide target closure geometries, whereas the specific RCP mapping from an RD aggregate to such a target is a separate hypothesis.

### 3.2 Boundary conditions

A target closure geometry imposes at least two distinguishable types of boundary condition.

**Positional condition.** The relevant boundary data must occupy the appropriate hyperplane(s). In a reduced representation this becomes a condition of the form

$$n_i \cdot x = d_i.$$

**Orientational condition.** The associated boundary or mirror normals must have the required relative orientations. For a reflection system generated by hyperplanes $H_i$, the angle between adjacent mirrors is directly related to the Coxeter relation

$$(r_i r_j)^{m_{ij}} = 1,$$

with

$$\alpha_{ij} = \frac{\pi}{m_{ij}}$$

for the relevant adjacent mirrors. *(Encyclopedia of Mathematics)*

Thus position and orientation are mathematically independent pieces of information and should not be collapsed into one definition of "closure."

## 4. Formal Statement of the Composite-Seed RCP Hypothesis

### 4.1 Positional claim

The positional component of the hypothesis is:

> An appropriately selected finite RD aggregate can reproduce the positional boundary conditions of a target four-dimensional closure geometry exactly or, where exactness is not possible, asymptotically as the aggregate resolution increases.

For the cases already computed, the stronger finite-resolution proposition appears to hold:

$$\epsilon_{\text{pos}}(L) = 0.$$

This should ultimately be proved analytically from the lattice and selection rule rather than established solely by numerical inspection.

### 4.2 Orientational claim

The stronger orientational proposition is:

> The microscopic facet-normal system of an RD aggregate can reproduce the orientational boundary conditions of the target four-dimensional closure geometry exactly or asymptotically as resolution increases.

The current cube and octahedron evidence points in the opposite direction.

If

$$\epsilon_{\text{ang}}(L) = \theta_0$$

for every tested $L$, with $\theta_0 > 0$, then

$$\lim_{L \to \infty} \epsilon_{\text{ang}}(L) = \theta_0 \neq 0.$$

That would rule out asymptotic orientational convergence under the specific fine-grained RCP interpretation being tested. It would not rule out:

- another projection;
- another choice of microscopic data;
- another RD-derived composite;
- or a different higher-dimensional structure generated by the fixed-angle mirrors.

## 5. Methodology

### 5.1 Cube selection

For the cube case, the aggregate is selected using an $L_\infty$-type rule,

$$|x|_\infty \le L,$$

or its precisely defined RD-lattice equivalent.

The resulting boundary consists of six principal directional classes. The critical test is whether the exposed facet centroids associated with each class satisfy a common plane equation.

### 5.2 Octahedral selection

For the octahedral case, an $L_1$-type selection rule is used:

$$|x|_1 \le L,$$

again interpreted on the actual RD centre lattice.

The eight boundary classes correspond to the sign-restricted diagonal directions. The positional test is identical in principle to the cube test, while the microscopic normal system is different.

### 5.3 Tetrahedral selection

The tetrahedral case requires greater care. A regular tetrahedron in a cubic coordinate frame can be represented by four sign-restricted $\langle111\rangle$ directions, for example

$$(1,1,1), \quad (1,-1,-1), \quad (-1,1,-1), \quad (-1,-1,1).$$

However, the RD facet-normal system is associated with a different directional family. Consequently, the tetrahedral experiment should not be specified merely as "the same pattern with a different angle."

Instead, the RCP construction should explicitly calculate

$$\theta_{\text{tet}} = \arccos\left(\frac{\nu \cdot n_{\text{tet}}}{|\nu||n_{\text{tet}}|}\right)$$

for every relevant RD facet normal $\nu$ and target tetrahedral direction $n_{\text{tet}}$. Only after that calculation should the existence, value, and constancy of a tetrahedral angular deficit be asserted.

## 6. Resolution Sweep

Let $L$ denote aggregate resolution. For each $L$, record:

**Positional residual**

$$\epsilon_{\text{pos}}(L) = \max_{c \in C_i} |n_i \cdot c - d_i|.$$

**Angular residual**

$$\epsilon_{\text{ang}}(L) = \max_{\nu \in N_i} \arccos\left(\frac{|\nu \cdot n_i|}{|\nu||n_i|}\right).$$

The absolute value is appropriate only if the application identifies opposite normals as equivalent; otherwise signed orientation should be retained separately.

The essential diagnostic is therefore not merely a plot of aggregate error, but two independent plots: $L \mapsto \epsilon_{\text{pos}}(L)$ and $L \mapsto \epsilon_{\text{ang}}(L)$.

The predicted cube pattern is $\epsilon_{\text{pos}}(L) = 0$ and $\epsilon_{\text{ang}}(L) = 45^\circ$.

The predicted octahedral pattern is $\epsilon_{\text{pos}}(L) = 0$ and $\epsilon_{\text{ang}}(L) = \arcsin(1/\sqrt{3})$.

These predictions require reproduction from the exact implementation before being promoted from computational observations to formal results.

## 7. Cube Case

### 7.1 Positional result

The current computation indicates that the exposed facet centroids associated with each cube-face direction are exactly coplanar at every tested resolution.

If the centre coordinates and facet-centroid offsets are indeed those specified in the RCP lattice model, this result admits a particularly simple explanation. The exposed-centroid coordinate is generated by a discrete boundary-selection rule. A max-norm boundary selects cells according to a fixed coordinate layer, while the RD facet-centroid offset is itself discrete. Consequently, all exposed centroids belonging to one directional class can inherit a common coordinate.

This is not conventional numerical convergence. It is an exact combinatorial boundary condition.

### 7.2 Angular result

The reported cube result is

$$\theta_{\text{cube}} = 45^\circ.$$

If confirmed analytically, this would mean that increasing $L$ changes the number and extent of the microscopic facets without changing their angular relationship to the effective cube-face direction. Thus $\epsilon_{\text{ang}}(L) = 45^\circ$ for all tested resolutions.

The result would therefore be fundamentally different from ordinary discretization error, where one expects $\epsilon(L) \to 0$.

### 7.3 Coarse interpretation

Under the coarse interpretation, the exposed centroid sets define an effective cube. The effective seed therefore satisfies the positional boundary conditions exactly. This is the sense in which the RD aggregate can be described as producing a composite seed.

The word "composite" is important: the effective face is not itself a single RD facet. It is an emergent boundary assembled from many microscopic cells.

### 7.4 Fine interpretation

Under the fine interpretation, every RD facet is retained as an independent geometric element. The relevant question is no longer "Do the centroids lie on the correct effective face?" but "Do the individual facet mirrors possess the required orientations?"

For the reported cube result, the answer would be no, because the angular offset remains $45^\circ$. This distinction should be one of the central results of the paper.

## 8. Octahedral Case

### 8.1 Positional result

The octahedral construction reportedly exhibits the same positional phenomenon. The exposed centroids belonging to an octahedral boundary class occupy an exact common plane. Thus, subject to verification from the explicit coordinate rules,

$$\epsilon_{\text{pos}}(L) = 0.$$

The underlying mechanism is again expected to be combinatorial: the $L_1$-type boundary selection fixes a discrete linear coordinate on the selected boundary layer.

### 8.2 Angular result

The reported angular offset is

$$\theta_{\text{oct}} = \arcsin\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt{3}}\right) \approx 35.2643897^\circ.$$

This number should be derived explicitly from the actual target normal and actual RD facet-normal vectors. It should not be justified merely by noting that $1/\sqrt{3}$ occurs naturally in cubic geometry.

If the derivation confirms the value, the resolution sweep should then establish whether $\epsilon_{\text{ang}}(L) = 35.2643897^\circ$ for all $L$. That would provide a second independent example of the same structural phenomenon.

---

## 9. Comparison of Cube and Octahedron

The important common result is not the numerical equality of the angular deficits — they are different. It is the structural form:

$$\epsilon_{\text{pos}}(L) = 0 \qquad \text{while} \qquad \epsilon_{\text{ang}}(L) = \theta_0 > 0.$$

This gives a useful conceptual distinction:

| Property | Coarse seed | Fine facet system |
|---|---|---|
| Positional closure | Exact | inherited from boundary |
| Orientation | Effective boundary orientation | Microscopic RD orientation |
| Resolution dependence | None after exact selection | None in reported cases |
| Limit $L \to \infty$ | Exact | Fixed angular deficit |

The result therefore cannot properly be summarized simply as "the RD construction closes" or "the RD construction fails." Both statements would discard essential information.

The more precise conclusion is:

> The RD aggregate can encode an exact effective boundary while its microscopic geometry retains a non-vanishing orientational discrepancy.

## 10. Coxeter and Reflection-Group Interpretation

### 10.1 Why the angular question matters

The orientational problem becomes substantially more interesting if the microscopic facets are interpreted as mirrors.

A reflection group generated by hyperplane reflections is governed by relations $(r_i r_j)^{m_{ij}} = 1$. For adjacent mirrors, the corresponding dihedral angle is $\alpha_{ij} = \pi/m_{ij}$. The Coxeter matrix therefore records the orders of products of generating reflections. *(Encyclopedia of Mathematics)*

The standard finite four-dimensional reflection groups include the groups associated with the regular 4-polytopes. In particular, $A_4 = [3,3,3]$, $B_4 = [4,3,3]$, and $F_4 = [3,4,3]$. The 8-cell and 16-cell belong to the $B_4$ system, while the 24-cell belongs to $F_4$. *(Kiwix Server)*

### 10.2 The cube's 45° result

The cube value $45^\circ = \pi/4$ is immediately compatible with a Coxeter relation having $m = 4$.

This does not establish that the complete RD-derived system is a finite Coxeter group. It establishes only that this particular pairwise angular relationship is individually compatible with a Coxeter-style reflection relation. The full question requires the complete set of mirrors and their pairwise relationships.

### 10.3 The octahedral angle

The value $\arcsin(1/\sqrt{3})$ does not immediately present itself as $\pi/m$ for an integer $m$. That makes it qualitatively different from the cube's 45° case.

But this still does not establish that the resulting reflection system is infinite. A complete reflection-group calculation is required. The relevant object is the full Gram/cosine matrix, not one isolated angle. Finite Coxeter groups can be characterized through positive-definiteness of the appropriate cosine matrix, and finite Coxeter groups are equivalent to finite real reflection groups in the standard setting. *(RWTH Aachen University Math Department)*

## 11. The Latent-Geometry Question

The most interesting consequence of the coarse/fine distinction is that failure of the fine system to reproduce the intended 4D polytope does not necessarily make the microscopic system geometrically uninteresting.

It creates a second research question:

> What geometric structure, if any, is generated by the complete RD-derived mirror system?

This is distinct from asking whether the system reproduces the standard tesseract, 24-cell, or another known regular 4-polytope. The two questions should therefore be separated.

**Question A — Composite-seed reconstruction.** Can the RD aggregate reproduce the effective boundary conditions of a known 4D closure geometry?

**Question B — Latent reflection structure.** Do the microscopic RD-derived hyperplanes themselves generate a finite reflection group, a discrete infinite group, an affine/hyperbolic analogue, or no discrete reflection group at all?

Question A can have a positive answer while Question B has a completely different answer.

## 12. Proposed Reflection-Group Experiment

For each finite aggregate:

**Step 1 — Construct all microscopic facet hyperplanes.** Represent every exposed facet as $H_i = \{x \mid n_i \cdot x = d_i\}$.

**Step 2 — Lift into the RCP representation.** Apply the explicitly defined RCP map to obtain the corresponding 4D hyperplanes.

**Step 3 — Determine pairwise angles.** Calculate

$$\cos\alpha_{ij} = \frac{|n_i \cdot n_j|}{|n_i||n_j|}.$$

**Step 4 — Test Coxeter compatibility.** Where $\alpha_{ij} = \pi/m_{ij}$ for integer $m_{ij}$, record the corresponding Coxeter relation. Where no such integer exists, record the pair as non-Coxeter under the ordinary finite Coxeter interpretation.

**Step 5 — Construct the Gram/cosine matrix.** For candidate generators, $G_{ij} = -\cos(\pi/m_{ij})$. More generally, use the directly measured normal-angle matrix.

**Step 6 — Test definiteness and rank.** The resulting matrix provides a principled test for whether the proposed generator system is compatible with a finite spherical reflection group. *(Encyclopedia of Mathematics)*

**Step 7 — Orbit test.** Starting from a generic seed point, repeatedly apply the candidate reflections. Measure: number of unique orbit points; growth with iteration depth; recurrence; stabilizers; fundamental-domain structure. A finite orbit under the generated group would provide strong evidence for finiteness, while unbounded orbit growth would support an infinite-group interpretation.

## 13. Tetrahedral Case

The tetrahedral case should remain explicitly preliminary.

A regular tetrahedron can be represented by four $\langle111\rangle$-type vectors with an appropriate sign restriction. The RD system, however, is naturally structured around a different directional family.

Therefore the correct experimental question is:

$$\theta_{\text{tet}} = \angle(\nu_{\text{RD}}, n_{\text{tet}}) = ?$$

for every relevant pair.

Three possibilities should remain open:

1. a single constant non-zero angle;
2. several discrete angular classes;
3. a more complicated distribution.

Only the computation can distinguish these. If the result again consists of a single resolution-independent angular value, the "two flat lines" phenomenon becomes substantially more compelling as a general property of the RD construction.

## 14. What Has Actually Been Established?

At the present stage, the claims should be divided into four categories.

### 14.1 Established mathematical background

These are standard facts:

- the RD is a 12-faced space-filling polyhedron;
- standard coordinates can be given using the $(\pm1,\pm1,\pm1)$ and axial $(\pm2,0,0)$-type vertices;
- the RD is associated with FCC geometry;
- the six convex regular 4-polytopes include the 5-cell, 8-cell, 16-cell, 24-cell, 120-cell and 600-cell;
- their symmetry systems include the finite Coxeter groups $A_4$, $B_4$, $F_4$, and $H_4$;
- finite reflection groups are governed by their reflection generators and Coxeter relations. *(MathWorld)*

### 14.2 Results apparently obtained computationally

These are the claims that should be documented with the actual RCP implementation:

- cube centroid planes are exact;
- octahedron centroid planes are exact;
- cube microscopic angular offset is $45^\circ$;
- octahedral microscopic angular offset is approximately $35.2644^\circ$;
- these values remain constant over the tested resolution range.

### 14.3 Results requiring analytic proof

The strongest version of the paper should prove $\epsilon_{\text{pos}}(L) = 0$ from the lattice-selection rule. It should also derive the angular values directly from the underlying vectors rather than infer them from numerical measurements.

### 14.4 Open hypotheses

The following remain open:

- the tetrahedral angular result;
- generality across other RD selection rules;
- whether the microscopic system defines a finite reflection group;
- whether it defines a non-standard 4D polytope;
- whether it defines an infinite but discrete reflection system;
- whether another 4D structure is naturally associated with the RD $\langle110\rangle$ geometry.

## 15. Revised Interpretation of the Composite-Seed Hypothesis

The strongest defensible formulation at this stage is therefore:

> The Composite-Seed RCP Hypothesis proposes that finite RD aggregates can encode the positional boundary conditions of selected four-dimensional closure geometries through emergent composite boundary surfaces, even when the microscopic RD facets do not individually reproduce the target orientational system.

This is stronger scientifically than simply claiming that RD cells "make" a 4D polytope because it specifies exactly what is being reproduced.

The hypothesis naturally divides into two propositions:

**Proposition P1 — Positional composite closure.** $\epsilon_{\text{pos}}(L) = 0$ for the tested boundary-selection constructions.

**Proposition P2 — Microscopic orientational closure.** $\lim_{L \to \infty} \epsilon_{\text{ang}}(L) = 0$ for the target reflection system.

The current cube and octahedron observations support P1 and appear inconsistent with P2.

But P2 should be stated as refuted only for the tested RCP/facet interpretation, not as a universal impossibility theorem about every conceivable RD-based construction.

## 16. Discussion

### 16.1 The significance of the two flat lines

The phrase "two flat lines" captures the central empirical result unusually well.

A conventional discretization error would normally have the form $\epsilon(L) \to 0$ as resolution increases. Here the proposed pattern is instead $\epsilon_{\text{position}}(L) = 0$ from the beginning, while $\epsilon_{\text{orientation}}(L) = \theta_0$.

The two errors therefore belong to fundamentally different categories. The positional result arises from the selection combinatorics of the lattice. The orientational result arises from the intrinsic geometry of the RD facet. Increasing resolution changes the quantity of geometric material but not the orientation of the microscopic building block.

### 16.2 Coarse closure is not approximate closure

This distinction deserves emphasis. If all exposed centroids lie exactly on an effective boundary plane, then describing the resulting seed as "approximately planar" would actually understate the result.

The correct statement is: the sampled boundary data are exactly coplanar, subject to the coordinate and selection definitions. The approximation enters only when that composite surface is identified with a higher-dimensional target structure.

### 16.3 Fine failure does not imply geometric failure

Similarly, a fixed angular discrepancy does not mean the RD system is geometrically defective. It means that two different structures are being compared: the target mirror system versus the RD microscopic mirror system.

The latter may possess its own mathematically coherent structure. This motivates the latent-group experiment rather than ending the investigation after observing the angular mismatch.

## 17. Limitations

Several limitations should be made explicit.

**17.1 Tetrahedral case.** The tetrahedral calculation has not yet been established and should not be presented as a result.

**17.2 RCP definition.** The paper requires a complete mathematical definition of the RCP mapping. A verbal description is insufficient if the result is intended to be independently reproducible.

**17.3 Numerical evidence.** A resolution sweep demonstrates empirical constancy but does not by itself prove constancy for all $L$.

**17.4 Angular derivation.** The reported $45^\circ$ and $35.2644^\circ$ values require direct vector derivations.

**17.5 Reflection-group conclusion.** No finite-group conclusion should be drawn until the complete microscopic hyperplane system has been constructed and its Gram/Coxeter structure analysed.

## 18. Conclusions

The present investigation supports a sharper formulation of the Composite-Seed RCP problem.

The key observation is that positional and orientational closure are separable. For the tested cube and octahedron constructions, the available computational evidence indicates that the exposed RD facet centroids can reproduce the corresponding effective boundary planes exactly at finite resolution.

At the same time, the microscopic RD facet normals appear to retain fixed angular offsets from the target boundary directions. The reported values are $45^\circ$ for the cube construction and approximately $35.2644^\circ$ for the octahedral construction, subject to formal derivation and reproducibility.

Consequently, the central result should not presently be expressed as simply "RD aggregates reproduce 4D closure." Nor should it be expressed simply as "RD aggregates fail to reproduce 4D closure."

The more precise statement is:

> RD aggregates may reproduce the positional boundary conditions of a four-dimensional closure geometry through an emergent composite seed while failing to reproduce the microscopic orientational boundary conditions of that same target geometry.

This establishes a meaningful distinction between coarse composite closure and fine orientational closure.

The next question is consequently no longer merely whether increasing resolution eliminates the angular discrepancy. If the discrepancy is analytically shown to be resolution-independent, that question is settled for the tested construction.

The more interesting question becomes:

> What mathematical structure is generated by the fixed-angle RD microscopic mirror system itself?

That question leads naturally to a reflection-group analysis based on the complete hyperplane arrangement, its Coxeter/Gram data, orbit growth, and fundamental-domain structure.

## 19. Recommended Next Steps

**Priority 1 — Formalize RCP.** Write the exact coordinate map $\mathcal{R}: (x,y,z,\text{cell/facet data}) \rightarrow \text{RCP boundary data}$. This is the foundation of the entire argument.

**Priority 2 — Prove the positional result.** For the cube and octahedron, derive analytically why the selected facet centroids satisfy their respective boundary equations exactly.

**Priority 3 — Derive the angular results.** Calculate the relevant RD facet normals and target normals explicitly. Establish $\theta_{\text{cube}} = \pi/4$ and independently determine whether $\theta_{\text{oct}} = \arcsin(1/\sqrt{3})$ is exactly the angle generated by the RCP construction.

**Priority 4 — Run the tetrahedral experiment.** Do not assume its result. Calculate the complete angular spectrum.

**Priority 5 — Perform the latent reflection-group experiment.** Construct the complete microscopic hyperplane system and calculate: pairwise angles; Coxeter candidates; Gram matrix; eigenvalues; group orbit; fundamental-domain structure; finite/infinite behaviour.

**Priority 6 — Only then make the broader claim.** If the cube, octahedron and tetrahedron all produce the same structural phenomenon,

$$\epsilon_{\text{position}} = 0, \qquad \epsilon_{\text{orientation}} = \text{constant} \neq 0,$$

then the evidence for a general RD-composite-seed principle becomes considerably stronger.
